\begin{abstract}
\noindent The original Internet was envisioned to become an open and distributed network that was scalable and fair, allowing access to data and services without surveillance or security concerns. However, in recent years, the Internet has become increasingly centralized, controlled and monopolized by big businesses running huge data centers. This centralization has given big entities and businesses unprecedented control of the traffic and data of the network.

\vspace{0.1cm}
\begin{mdframed}[style=textimage]
	\includegraphics[width=331pt]{lights}
\end{mdframed}

\noindent With a majority of resources on the Internet owned by a minority of big corporations, these businesses are able to maintain a high cost to value ratio. The centralization and lack of fault tolerance and redundancy makes your personal data less secured. As a remedy to this deteriorating trend, we suggest the inception of a decentralized and consensus-driven segmented blockchain network based on a striped storage solution. The protocol allows for a completely decentralized and secure blockchain-based Internet where any user, including private persons, can host a service node that aids the network with storage space, bandwidth and, in time, compute cycles, and is meant to earn an income for doing so. To allow for complete utilization of the network, an access layer is planned, allowing for the development of protocols, services, and infrastructure.
\vspace*{+40pt}
\end{abstract}

Amazon, Microsoft and Google together held 63\% of the worldwide market for cloud infrastructure services in the second quarter of 2026, a market worth \$143 billion that quarter alone. A month later, Cloudflare, which many websites rely on to reach their users, suffered what it called its worst outage since 2019 \cite{cloudflare2025}. If your provider goes offline or gets disrupted - your service and data become unreachable.

\noindent The centralized nature of the resource ownership on the Internet also results in under-utilization of servers, with idle CPU cycles and gigabytes or potentially terabytes of unused storage space on a single server. This is especially true when businesses choose to cost-optimize their operations and rely on their own dedicated servers, rather than relying on the more expensive cloud provider solutions.

Internet freedom has declined worldwide for fifteen years in a row, and in countries such as China and Myanmar, censorship is so prevalent that the public is limited to the news sources it is allowed to access and view \cite{freedomhouse2025}. For privacy, cost and security reasons, this situation is not sustainable. Hackers, oppressive governments and monopolizing businesses continuously develop better tools that infringe on your data and privacy - creating a very vulnerable situation.

\section{Discussion}
The primary goal of Unigrid is to implement an anonymous and decentralized communication solution that offers data storage and compute cycle utilization. Private individuals and businesses that value privacy or require secure data storage can take advantage of this feature.

As an example, sensitive documents such as medical records can be stored on the network in encrypted form, readable only by whoever holds each file's key, its fingerprint. Today, sharing a fingerprint shares full control of the file, the right to delete it included; read-only and expiring access could be built on top of it. It should be the owning individual or business that controls how their private data is accessed and shared. It should never be in the control of a third party - something that is usually the case today. When the data is controlled by a third party, the risk of information leaks increases dramatically. Sometimes data is stolen by hackers, other times it is leaked or shared on purpose (in secrecy or otherwise). Whatever the reason - the Unigrid network gives the control back to the user or the party that actually owns the data.

\noindent The Internet of Things (IoT) is growing rapidly: the number of connected IoT devices reached about 21 billion at the end of 2025 and is expected to reach 39 billion by 2030 \cite{iotanalytics2025}. This growth introduces a wealth of opportunities for innovation and improved connectivity. However, this expansion is not without its challenges. A pressing issue in the IoT realm is ensuring the security and integrity of data as devices, often from different manufacturers and utilizing distinct protocols, exchange information. Traditional security models sometimes struggle to offer foolproof solutions in such a complex environment.

Blockchain technology emerges as a beacon in this scenario. Within a blockchain framework, the ledger is meticulously maintained to be secure, with each entry being resistant to unauthorized alterations. This decentralized approach effectively removes the conventional need for absolute trust between communicating devices. When IoT systems integrate with a blockchain network, such as Unigrid, they inherit an augmented layer of security, ensuring that the data not only remains trustworthy but also significantly less vulnerable to potential breaches.

\noindent The sheer volume of devices connecting to IoT presents another challenge: scalability. It's not just about preserving the integrity of data; the network must also support vast numbers of devices without compromising performance or speed. A surge in the number of IoT devices means the infrastructure must adapt quickly and efficiently. As noted by Shruti Jain from Deloitte \cite{jain2021}, blockchain and distributed ledger technology (DLT) present promising avenues to tackle these IoT security and scalability issues, ensuring a more interconnected and secure future.

\subsection{Gridnodes}
A gridnode is similar in function to a masternode as initially conceived by the Darkcoin project, where anybody can set-up a node on a server by locking up a certain amount of tokens to that node. These masternodes then provide a service to the network by contributing network bandwidth in the form of helping other nodes to synchronize and verify blocks on the network. Because these masternodes secure the network in this way, they are rewarded in tokens.

The Unigrid network takes this idea further with gridnodes. Each gridnode signs its own entry in the network's list of gridnodes with its key and renews it every few minutes, so an entry that is no longer renewed soon drops out. Gridnodes are planned to be scored by the quality of the service they provide to the network and its nodes: latency, available storage capacity, processing speed and network bandwidth are all meant to be taken into account, and each gridnode ranked against the others.

With a regular masternode, reward frequency and occurrence is based on a naively calculated list. The network simply stores this list into memory and will cycle through each block to select the next winner. With the Unigrid network and gridnodes, scoring and rewards are planned to work differently, taking into account which gridnode received the last reward and the scores of each gridnode. Where data is stored does not depend on such a winner. The owner's own node encrypts a file, cuts it into pieces and sends the pieces of each chunk to gridnodes chosen by a hash of the chunk's name, so each gridnode keeps at most one piece of any chunk and the network scales with the number of gridnodes (see Data Storage).

\subsection{Side Chains}
The Unigrid network places blocks in side chains suited to the type of work a communicating node sends. Each side chain is controlled by a group of gridnodes, a shard group. A side chain is a specialized blockchain with very specific characteristics, optimized for the type of work it is meant for. A direct communication channel needs brute speed, while a ledger of value must be beyond doubt, so the Unigrid network treats these workloads differently. The communication side chains, in development for the network's proxy (see Migration), are small and kept in memory. Their entries are hashed with a very fast but less collision resistant algorithm and signed by whoever writes them. The ledger, also in development, makes the opposite trade: its blocks are hashed with SHA-512 and count only once more than two thirds of its validators have certified them. That makes it very secure, but it can not handle the same throughput as the in-memory chains. Chains for compute cycles are planned to follow the same principle.

\noindent The Unigrid network creates side chains on demand. The shard groups and their communication chains are derived anew at the start of every epoch, an hour by default, from the network's settings, the list of gridnodes and the time, so they follow the gridnodes as they come and go. Entries are dropped once they are older than the retention time the network sets, so a communication chain never grows without bound.

\subsection{Data Storage}
The Unigrid network stores files on its gridnodes in a way that is trust-minimized and verifiable: no single gridnode has to be trusted with a file, and anyone can check that what a gridnode holds is genuine. A file is encrypted on its owner's own node before anything leaves it. One random secret, the fingerprint, both locates and decrypts the file, and the network keeps no other record of who stored what. The encrypted file is cut into chunks and protected by two layers of erasure coding: an outer layer whose structure only the owner knows, and an inner layer that lets the gridnodes check and rebuild every chunk on their own, without the owner and, except in short stripes, without learning which chunks belong together. The design, its analysis and its known limits are described in the storage white paper \cite{storage2026}.

\noindent The cloud storage market was worth an estimated \$161 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach \$810 billion by 2034 \cite{fort2026}. It might grow even bigger if networks such as Unigrid reach general adoption. Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs) are tokens where only one specific token can be in existence at a given time. These allow for proof of ownership of a digital asset. The problem with the current system is that the only thing being stored on-chain is the actual proof of ownership. The asset itself that an NFT is tethered to is actually stored on a normal web server or some other means of storage. Consequently, if that web server or storage shuts down or is moved, the data for that NFT is lost.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\begin{mdframed}[style=textimage]
	\includegraphics[width=381pt]{hard-drive}

\noindent The Unigrid network is well suited to NFTs and other digital assets. Because the gridnodes repair lost pieces on their own, the asset data survives the loss of a large share of the machines that hold it, and nobody without its fingerprint can remove it. Until read-only access exists, though, sharing an asset's fingerprint also shares the right to delete it. This gives assets, which in some cases cost millions of dollars, a more durable home than a single web server.

The NFT market has cooled since the boom of 2021, but it remains large: NFT sales came to about \$5.6 billion in 2025, down 37\% from the year before \cite{cointelegraphNft2025}. Every one of those assets still needs somewhere to live.

\subsection{Sharding \& Parity Blocks}
Keeping a full copy of every file on many machines is wasteful, and keeping it on one is fragile. The Unigrid network cuts each encrypted file into chunks of one mebibyte and each chunk into fragments, and adds parity at both levels: extra pieces computed from the others. A file gains half as many parity chunks as it has data chunks, and any 16 of a chunk's 24 fragments rebuild it. A file costs about 2.3 times its size on the network and survives far more loss than three full copies would. The result is a network built for durability, privacy and scalability.

\noindent Sharding is a database architecture pattern related to horizontal partitioning - the practice of separating one table’s rows into multiple different tables, known as partitions \cite{mark2019}. On the Unigrid network, the fragments of each chunk are spread over a set of gridnodes chosen by a hash of the chunk's name. Every node can work out where a chunk lives without a directory, and the chunks of one file land on unrelated gridnodes. The gridnodes of each group take turns to count the fragments that remain and rebuild the missing ones once too many are gone, so the data stays alive while its owner is offline.

A video streaming service could build on this redundancy. A reader asks a few more gridnodes than it needs, 18 for the 16 fragments of a chunk, and uses the first answers, so up to two slow or missing gridnodes do not hold it up. The service can rely on the network's own repair instead of running replication of its own, which makes it simpler and cheaper to build than a more traditional solution.

\subsection{Compute Cycles}
Compute is a longer-term goal of the network and not yet part of the node software. As the network grows and more gridnodes come online, the compute power available on the network will continuously increase. The scoring algorithms on the network will encourage operators to run gridnodes with a lot of resources - resulting in a network with a constantly increasing storage capacity and available computational power. Using Unigrid tokens, organizations and users will be able to rent these resources and use them for custom workloads and data.

\vspace{0.12cm}
\begin{mdframed}[style=textimage]
	\includegraphics[width=381pt]{compute}
\end{mdframed}

\noindent One use case appropriate for this would be a scientific study that needs to run some very complex computationally intensive tasks. In return, the gridnodes offering the service would be awarded a certain amount of Unigrid, as decided by the network. 

Another way to take advantage of the computational power on the network would be to run a container or VPS (Virtual Private Server) on top of the gridnodes. Built on the same redundancy as storage, the network would allow for the deployment of containers and server instances that never go offline unintentionally.

After the CPU, the Unigrid network is to support GPU workloads. This means that cycles specifically written for graphical processors will also be handled by the gridnodes on the network. As an even more distant goal and when the available technology allows for a working implementation, the foundation also plans to add support for quantum cycles - which would allow the network to process work specifically designed for quantum processors.

\subsection{Migration}
Connecting directly to the ordinary Internet exposes your personal information to external parties. In order to avoid this leaking of personal data, many Internet users use a VPN (Virtual Private Network). This helps to protect your personal data by creating an encrypted tunnel to the Internet. However, the question is - can you fully trust the VPN provider?

\vspace{0.05cm}
\begin{mdframed}[style=textimage]
	\includegraphics[width=381pt]{migration}
\end{mdframed}

\noindent When accessing the ordinary Internet via the Unigrid network, an application connects to a SOCKS5 proxy served by the user's own node, and each connection gets a fingerprint of its own. The connection travels over a communication side chain of a shard group, and the gridnode chosen by a hash of that fingerprint acts as the “outlet” that reaches the destination. Everything between the user's node and the outlet is encrypted, so the gridnodes that carry it can not read it. No provider sits in the middle and no single company runs the outlets: the gridnodes act as a swarm of proxies.

The proxy is in development, and its limits are worth stating. Like the end of any proxy or VPN, the outlet sees what it relays to the destination, so applications should still use TLS. The traffic takes a single hop, so the proxy hides it from the local network but does not yet make its user anonymous: the gridnodes a user connects to see the user's address, though not what is sent. This is very different from Tor (onion routing), which sends data through a chain of hops to hide who is talking to whom. An anonymous communication layer remains a goal of the network, and a very important use case for new users.

\subsection{Governance}
The Unigrid Foundation manages the network. Network-wide settings, such as the parameters of storage, are carried by sporks: signed settings records that every node checks for itself. A settings spork takes effect only when two of the four keys held by the foundation's board have signed it, so no single person can change the network alone, and every change leaves a signed, chained record of who approved it. Through sporks the foundation tunes the way the implementations and algorithms of the network behave, and it is meant to be able to blacklist blocks the same way. Sporks set parameters and nothing else: they never touch a user's data, and the network keeps working while the foundation is offline.

\vspace{0.05cm}
\begin{mdframed}[style=textimage]
	\includegraphics[width=381pt]{abstract-particles}
\end{mdframed}

\noindent For selected changes the foundation will be able to ask the network instead: it will put a proposal to a vote of the network and steer the process. Which changes go to a vote depends on the spork; a change of the maximum supply is meant to be one of them. Voting is not yet part of the node software. Every node operator also chooses which keys their node trusts, so the foundation's authority rests on operators continuing to follow it.

\subsection{Past Decentralization Attempts}
Numerous projects in the digital landscape champion the notion of decentralization, promoting it as a key feature of their networks. However, upon closer examination, it becomes evident that the majority have limitations that deviate from the core principles of true decentralization. Many such projects necessitate the use of specialized hardware, creating barriers to entry for the average user. Furthermore, instead of promoting an open and inclusive environment, they restrict participation by implementing systems like written applications and rigorous vetting processes.

This approach does not genuinely eliminate centralization; it merely shifts it. Rather than distributing power and control across the network, they simply relocate the centralization to a different point, inadvertently introducing new potential vulnerabilities and points of failure. This contradicts the essence of decentralization, which is to prevent the concentration of power and to eliminate single points of failure.

Unigrid stands out by taking a fundamentally different approach. It envisions a system where barriers are minimized, and inclusivity is maximized. With Unigrid, participation isn't just a possibility—it's an invitation. Anyone, regardless of their technical background or resources, can join the network and take on the role of a gridnode host. This not only broadens the base of participants but also strengthens the network's resilience and reach. Gridnodes can also be given a voice: the foundation can put proposals and network changes to a vote of the network, so that the network evolves together with its community.

\subsection{Competitive Advantage}
The Unigrid network will keep the cost of data storage and data processing to a minimum. With gridnodes constantly competing for work, costs will be drastically lower than alternative services.

\noindent Data on the network is encrypted, coded with redundancy and spread across the gridnodes, which rebuild whatever is lost. No single machine can take the data offline, and nobody but the owner can delete it.

\noindent The difference that matters is structural. A provider whose business is holding its customers' data cannot offer that without giving up the business.

\subsection{Privacy by Design}
Privacy-enhancing technologies go further than anonymization, which strips names from data but often leaves the people in it open to re-identification. Unigrid storage builds privacy into its structure:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Encryption at the source.} Files are encrypted on the owner's node, and gridnodes only ever hold ciphertext.
	\item \textbf{Data minimization.} The network keeps no accounts, owner records or file names.
	\item \textbf{Unlinkability.} The pieces of one file are scattered over unrelated gridnodes, and the network keeps no record of which belong together.
	\item \textbf{Access by capability.} Holding the fingerprint is the only right to a file; no account or administrator can grant or revoke it.
	\item \textbf{Erasure.} A signed delete tells every gridnode that holds a piece of the file to drop it, and destroying the fingerprint leaves any copy that remains unreadable.
\end{itemize}

\noindent These properties follow the principle of data protection by design and by default that the GDPR sets out \cite{gdpr2016}. For the European digital single market they make Unigrid a framework in which control over data rests on the protocol rather than on a provider's policy, something that platforms built on collecting and analyzing data cannot easily copy.

\subsection{Responsibility \& Jurisdiction}
Who can do what on the network is the basis on which legal responsibility can be assessed. The owner decides what to store and alone can read or delete it. Gridnode operators run machines that hold encrypted fragments without any key: they cannot see what they store, and so cannot judge or flag it. The Unigrid Foundation, a Swedish foundation under the supervision of a county administrative board \cite{stiftelselag}, sets the network's parameters, and that role gives it no access to users' data. Fragments may lie in any country where gridnodes run. The storage white paper sets out the technical facts that bear on data protection law, and placement restricted by the owner, for example to gridnodes in the European Union, is planned \cite{storage2026}.

\subsection{Gridnode Incentives}
Gridnodes are to be rewarded for the service they provide (see Gridnodes and Tokenomics). For storage, the incentives build on mechanisms the node software already has. Each gridnode holds at most one piece of any chunk, and only as much as its quota allows, although until collateral is enforced one operator can run many gridnodes. Gridnodes check each other's claims with random spot checks, and the network repairs whatever a dishonest gridnode drops. Planned on top of this are collateral bound to each gridnode's key, so that running many gridnodes costs stake that can be lost; a reputation built from failed spot checks and refused stores; and payment for storage held over time, settled on the network's ledger. The aim is that storing data honestly pays better than any way of cheating.

\subsection{Why Java}
Hedgehog, the node software, is written in Java, and the choice is deliberate. Java remains one of the most widely used languages for server and infrastructure software: Apache Kafka is written mainly in Java, and Apache Cassandra and the Ethereum client Besu almost entirely \cite{kafkaRepo,cassandraRepo,besuDocs}. Its long-term support releases receive years of updates: Java 25, released in September 2025, is supported until at least September 2030, with extended support until 2033 \cite{oracleJavaRoadmap}. Few languages have a larger pool of developers. Memory safety protects nodes that must parse data sent by strangers. Hedgehog ships as one executable per platform for Linux, macOS on Apple Silicon and Windows, a launcher built with GraalVM Native Image that carries its own trimmed Java runtime, so operators install nothing else \cite{graalvmNativeImage}. Languages such as Rust or Go have strengths of their own, but the storage format does not depend on the language, so other implementations can join the network beside this one.

The foundation is overseen by the county administrative board of the region, helping to ensure that assets are properly managed and that the foundation charter is adhered to.

\subsection{Sale Participation \& Early Token Holders}
The Unigrid project, a visionary undertaking in the decentralized landscape, is currently in its early stages, laying the groundwork for what could be a transformative initiative. As it navigates the initial challenges and milestones, The Unigrid Foundation plays a crucial role in its trajectory. The true essence of the Unigrid project lies not just in its technology but in its approach to decision-making. By integrating a blend of technological innovation with democratic governance the network exemplifies a forward-thinking approach, setting a standard for other decentralized endeavours to follow.

\section{Tokenomics}
Throughout the evolution of Unigrid, our tokenomics have undergone meticulous refinement across several iterations. In 2023 they were developed in collaboration with the partners ValueX \cite{valuex2023} and NFMedia \cite{nfmedia2023}, whose insights and expertise helped shape the framework. In 2024 the total supply was reduced from 150 million to 100 million tokens. The cut came out of the foundation's pool and the sale rounds still to come, so no existing holder lost any tokens.

The primary objective behind our tokenomics has always been to establish a resilient ecosystem. Protecting the market is a top priority, ensuring a stable environment that is resistant to manipulative practices and unforeseen market disruptions.

The value of our token isn't solely monetary; it represents the utility and functionality within the Unigrid system. By developing a tokenomic structure that promotes stability, we aim to ensure that the token remains consistently reliable in fulfilling its intended purpose within our network.

Furthermore, our gridnode operators are essential pillars of the Unigrid ecosystem. Their continuous efforts and contributions are invaluable, and our tokenomics are thus designed to recognize and reward their commitment. This approach ensures they are adequately incentivized and that their interests align with the overall health and growth of the network.

Tokens held on the earlier Unigrid blockchain, the legacy chain, are not left behind. Its balances have been captured in a snapshot signed by the foundation, which every Hedgehog node can check for itself. The ledger of Hedgehog, now in development, is built to let holders claim those balances with the keys of their legacy wallets.

\noindent In essence, through iterative design and with the collaboration of partners like ValueX and NFMedia, our tokenomics have been crafted to prioritize the stability and well-being of the market, the utility of the token, and the prosperity of the gridnode operators.

\section{Press Coverage \& Social Change}
The Unigrid Foundation recently won a court case in Sweden against the county of Västra Götaland, which is responsible for overseeing the registration of foundations. The crux of the matter revolved around the classification of cryptocurrencies as assets for foundations. Specifically, the county argued that merely separating crypto assets for a foundation did not fulfill the requisite duration requirement for the foundation's purpose. However, the Unigrid Foundation contended and successfully established in court that cryptocurrencies should be recognized as an accepted asset class during the formation of foundations~\cite{decentralized-internet,linkedin-paulk,linkedin-valuexag}.

This victory is seen as a major accomplishment not only for the Unigrid Foundation but also in paving the way for the future registration of foundations, potentially altering how crypto assets are perceived and handled in legal and foundational settings in Sweden and possibly beyond~\cite{linkedin-valuexag}.

\section{Conclusion}
Since the advent of the Internet in the 1960's \cite{int1997} the Internet can be defined as a collection of nodes (computers) communicating with each other. The Unigrid Foundation aims to decentralize and load-balance these nodes, allowing anyone to partake in being a host node on the Internet. From the first contact with the network, your data is encrypted and sharded, making it impossible for anyone else to access this data without a key. Browsing through the network keeps your traffic away from any single provider, and making it anonymous is the next goal - securing your privacy rights.
